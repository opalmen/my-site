One of the central puzzles in growth economics is how to measure something that, by its nature, cannot be owned. {{< sn id="sn1" note="This is distinct from the measurement of physical capital, where depreciation schedules and investment data give us at least a rough accounting. See [Corrado et al. (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1162/rest.91.4.875) for a systematic attempt to extend the capital concept to intangibles." >}}Knowledge spillovers{{< /sn >}} — the way that ideas developed in one firm or laboratory benefit others without any market transaction — have been invoked to explain everything from agglomeration economies to the persistence of growth.

The theoretical case is clean. Ideas, unlike objects, are non-rival: my use of an insight does not diminish your ability to use it. {{< sn id="sn2" note="Paul Romer's 1990 paper made this the fulcrum of endogenous growth theory. The non-rivalry of ideas means that production functions with knowledge inputs must exhibit increasing returns — a departure from the standard competitive framework." >}}Non-rivalry implies increasing returns{{< /sn >}}, which implies that competitive equilibrium cannot exist without some departure from the standard framework — a patent system, a university, a government laboratory.

## What we can and cannot observe

The empirical challenge is that spillovers are, almost by definition, transactions that leave no paper trail. We infer their presence indirectly: from {{< sn id="sn3" note="Jaffe (1989) was the first systematic study. Subsequent work by Jaffe, Trajtenberg, and Henderson (1993) used patent citations as a proxy for knowledge flows and found strong geographic localization — a finding that has held up across many replications." >}}patent citations{{< /sn >}}, from the geographic clustering of innovative activity, from production function estimates that show higher TFP in regions near research universities.

Each of these proxies has well-understood limitations. Patent citations reflect legal strategy as much as intellectual debt. Geographic clustering might be explained by input prices rather than knowledge flows. TFP residuals absorb all manner of unobserved heterogeneity.

## A modest proposal

Rather than searching for ever-better proxies, it may be worth asking whether the question itself is well-posed. "How large are knowledge spillovers?" may be less tractable — and less useful — than asking "under what institutional arrangements do knowledge flows produce the most broadly distributed economic benefit?"

This reframing shifts the object of inquiry from a quantity to a mechanism. It also connects the measurement problem to the policy problem in a more direct way. {{< sn id="sn4" note="This is roughly the move that Hausmann and Rodrik (2003) made in the context of industrial policy: rather than asking whether the state can 'pick winners,' asking what institutional form allows self-discovery to proceed with the fewest distortions." >}}The question is no longer how big the market failure is, but what kinds of institutions have historically corrected it.{{< /sn >}}
